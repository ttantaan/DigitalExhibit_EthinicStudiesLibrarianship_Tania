Statement of purpose

This list grew out of an undergraduate course I took at UCSB in 2016 on ghost and Gothic literature. I no longer have the syllabus, but I remember Bram Stoker’s Dracula , Edgar Allan Poe’s “The Fall of the House of Usher,” Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein , and the 1920s film Nosferatu . All of these works emerge from European or Euro-American traditions and emphasize the fear of the Other. This list centers the Other, speaking from within these marginalized communities.

Horror can attract readers who might otherwise reject or resist a conventional ethnic studies book list. The current political climate has created buzzwords that deter some people and institutions from engaging with concepts of diversity. Through suspense and vivid imagery, this list can engage an undergraduate audience with the experiences of disenfranchised communities.

These writers work in the space between the rational and the supernatural, where ancestors, dreams, and spirits are treated as meaningful sources of knowledge. Their stories raise a central ethnic studies question: whose epistemologies are treated as evidence, and whose are dismissed as superstition? The buried secrets that Gothic literature so often explores also resemble the recovery of histories that dominant narratives suppress.

What has society chosen to remember, and what has it chosen to forget?
